Level Devil:
what to learn,
what to build next
A source-backed benchmark for a wordless dad platformer set in
Indian cities, with a path to the wider world.
Prepared for Poshith and the development team • 23 November 2026
The design thesis
Make the player think “I understand what happened; one more try.” Protect that feeling with short
routes, dependable input, readable surprises and an affectionate comic lead. The setting must change
the puzzles, not only the backgrounds.
Decision
Recommendation
Initial product
One city, 15 original stages, three mechanical families, three buttons. Validate the loop before
building a world map.
Competitive edge
Clearer touch input, less repeated travel, dependable saves, restrained ads and a memorable
dad character.
Naming
Choose an independent name. “Level Devil Dad” presents substantial brand-confusion and
store-policy risk; see the legal assessment.
Evidence standard
Use sourced facts for the benchmark and explicitly proposed values for the new game. No
invented trigger coordinates, death averages or retention figures.
Evidence limit: forty archived layouts and a public reconstruction’s scene data, room scripts and C
controller were inspected. That adds specific trigger coordinates and timings for an older 80-room
sequence. It does not establish identical behaviour in today’s official mobile build. Live game/video
playback was unavailable here.
The first-40 cards distinguish reconstructed mechanics from inferred player reactions. Typical deaths,
actual second/third-attempt behaviour, current input latency and retention remain unverified. No original
game assets or implementation code are included.

Version discipline
The reference game is Adam Corey / Unept’s platformer, released in December 2023; Poki publishes the
web version. [4][12]
“Level” can mean a single room, a five-stage door or a whole world. Here, stage means one room and
door means its group. The first forty are original-world doors 1–8, as indexed in the 2024 walkthrough.
The screenshot archive has no dependable build stamp. Modern mobile, web and Steam updates must
not be assumed identical. [7][8]
Current mobile listings advertise 240+ levels. [2][3] Poki’s page gives inconsistent totals. Use dated build
inventories rather than treating marketing totals as an exact census. [1]
How to read the stage cards
R uses 16 px tiles, but a normal 7 px-wide collision body: one tile is not one character width. Δx is
spawn-to-trigger-anchor displacement, not distance until contact. Triggers fire on overlap. A1/A2/A3
are hypotheses; typical deaths remain unavailable. The coordinate appendix explains bounds and
scale. [28–31]

A useful interpretation of the curve
Early progression increases what the player must distrust: floor geometry → visible hazards → moving
supports → reward symbols → controls → time-dependent platforms → launch impulses → spatial
connections. That is a rise in reasoning burden as much as physical precision. This is an analytical
reading of the sequence, not a measured difficulty score.
A five-stage pattern to borrow as a method
Stage
Proposed role in an original door
1
Introduce a comprehensible interaction and a small surprise.
2
Move the same interaction to a new location; expose the lazy repetition.
3
Make the previous successful response insufficient for one visible reason.
4
Change the useful action: wait, retreat, walk or use the obstacle.
5
Combine two learned rules in a short capstone, then allow a clean celebration.
This is a proposed template, not the actual script of every door. Vary the position of the punchline.

How combinations increase depth
A moving support asks when?; a deceptive portal asks where?; an input reversal asks which button?
Teach each separately before combining two. The census shows how broad that vocabulary becomes:
saws and buttons are absent from the first forty authored scenes, yet each appears in fifteen of the
original eighty.
Controls 5 repeatedly changes a learned mapping. Platforms 4 undermines the learned return cadence
with a non-solid preview. These reconstructed examples demonstrate counter-adaptation: the player’s
previous correct inference becomes incomplete. [30]
Proposed 15-stage content budget
Use three primary families, five stages each. In each family: two single-rule rooms, two reversals, one
two-rule capstone. Across all fifteen, allow at most three capstones with two active rules at once. Do not
enforce a quota of deaths or a mandatory failure on every room.
For a real frequency study, count both stage presence and event instances. A room with six moving
spikes is one “moving-hazard stage” but may contain six entities and only one shared trigger. Those are
three different denominators.

A consistent first physics pass
Let u be the avatar collision width. Choose height H = 3u and airtime T = 0.70 s. For a constant-gravity
symmetric jump: g = 8H/T² ≈ 49u/s²; launch speed v₀ = 4H/T ≈ 17.14u/s. At a run speed of 9u/s,
range is 6.3u. These are computed design choices, not reverse-engineered constants.
If u = 24 logical pixels, this means run speed 216 px/s, jump height 72 px, launch speed about 411 px/s
and gravity about 1,176 px/s². Start with a fixed-height jump; add variable jump height only if tests show
that it improves control without obscuring trap logic.
Keep the controller trustworthy
Environmental rules may surprise. Dropped touches, drifting collision boxes and frame-rate-dependent
movement must not. Preserve a default controller and implement intentional rule changes as explicit
room states, with an obvious transition whenever possible.

Why a clip can work without words — analysis
The viewer can usually identify a body, an apparent route and a goal before understanding the controls.

Wordless humour and sharing — a design reading
Corey identifies humour as an objective and traces the idea to a retreating goal in Unicycle Legend. [4]

PROPOSED ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA / NOT MEASURED REFERENCE VALUES
20 numerical design rules — 1–10
01 · A readable goal
In a 2-second still-image test, at least 9 of 10 fresh viewers identify the controllable dad and the
destination. No text prompt.
02 · Immediate agency
Accept movement within 3 seconds of the title/play transition on the agreed test device; target the first
comic event within 10 seconds.
03 · Short solved routes
Target 6–12 seconds for an ordinary solved room and 15–25 seconds for a capstone. Time gameplay
separately from menus and ads.
04 · A small teaching unit
Introduce 1 primary new rule in a group of 5 stages. Use later stages to test it in new positions and with
one prior rule.
05 · A limited joke budget
Use 1–2 meaningful reversals in an ordinary room; cap a final stage at 3. More reveals require a
checkpoint or a separate room.
06 · Predictable simulation
Replay the same timestamped input 100 times from a fresh state: identical collision and outcome all 100
times. Cosmetic randomness may vary.
07 · A learnable response
After the player knows the rule, provide at least a 200 ms usable correction window in standard rooms.

08 · Little repeated labour

11 · Responsive touch
Touch-down to visible motion: P95 at most 83 ms, measured externally. Test simultaneous direction plus

12 · Forgiving edges

13 · Room to land
Ordinary landing surfaces at least 1.5 collision-body widths; optional precision challenges at least 1.1

14 · A death budget
Pilot medians: introductory rooms 1–3 deaths, regular rooms 2–6, capstones 4–10. Zero-death wins
remain valid; investigate a regular-room P90 above 15.
15 · A humane escape
Offer a hint after 5 deaths; offer a skip after 8 deaths or 90 seconds in one room. Never auto-skip or hide
the retry button.
16 · Protected momentum
No forced ad in the first 180 seconds; thereafter at most 1 per 180 active-play seconds, at a
completed-door boundary. Never on an ordinary death.
17 · Comfortable controls
Use 64–80 dp touch targets with 12–16 dp gaps as initial values. Let players reposition them; keep the
playfield out from under thumbs.
18 · Silent readability
At 360 px video width, at least 9 of 10 viewers should explain the visible cause of death after 2 viewings
with audio muted.
